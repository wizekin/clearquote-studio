STUDYMAP / PRACTICAL GUIDE

Build a balanced study timetable without overfilling your week

A timetable is useful when it makes the next session clear. Start with the time you actually have, then divide it among subjects. A full-looking calendar is not evidence of a realistic plan.

Count capacity before topics

Suppose you study on three days each week for two weeks, with two 40-minute sessions per day and a 10-minute break. That creates 12 focus sessions and 8 hours of study. Each study day occupies 90 minutes, because the break is between the two sessions.

Focus time: 3 days × 2 weeks × 2 sessions × 40 minutes = 480 minutes. Break time: 6 days × 10 minutes = 60 minutes.

Use priorities as relative weights

Mathematics at priority 3, Science at 2 and Writing at 1 divide 12 sessions into 6, 4 and 2. For each new session, StudyMap assigns the subject furthest behind its target share so far. Short plans involve rounding, and some low-priority subjects may receive no session when capacity is very limited.

Keep room for real work

The planner does not know assignment deadlines, prayer times, meals, classes, commutes or family commitments. Choose study days and a start time that leave space for these. A generated block is a place to decide what to work on, not a complete learning plan.

Import the calendar carefully

The calendar export uses local floating times. Import it into the calendar you normally use, inspect the result, and avoid importing the same file repeatedly. If you revise the plan, remove the old imported events before adding a replacement. CSV is a simple record if you prefer manual scheduling.

Review rather than accumulate

After a week, compare the plan with the sessions you could complete. Reduce the number of daily blocks if the plan was consistently too full. Adjust subject weights based on your own priorities; this tool does not infer academic need or predict results.
